Most theorems are “if–then” statements, so the conditional is the connective you'll prove most often. It's also the one whose everyday meaning drifts furthest from the mathematical one. This page pins it down.

Both connectives
The promise test

When is a promise broken?

A parent says: “If you clean your room, you get ice cream.” That's P → Q. Check each of the four situations and decide whether the promise was broken.

Only one situation breaks it: room cleaned, no ice cream. If the room wasn't cleaned, the promise was never tested, so it wasn't broken. That's why P → Q counts as true whenever P is false.

Translating

Six ways to say P → Q

The hard ones are “only if” and “necessary”. A trick: the arrow always points away from “if”, and toward “only if”.

If P then QP → Q
Q if PP → Q
P only if QP → Q

You can be P only if you're Q: P can't happen without Q.

P is sufficient for QP → Q

P is enough to guarantee Q.

Q is necessary for PP → Q

You can't have P without Q.

P unless Q¬Q → P

If not Q, then P. Equivalently, P ∨ Q.

Relatives

Converse and contrapositive

From P → Q you can make the converse Q → P and the contrapositive ¬Q → ¬P.

The contrapositive is always equivalent to the original: “if it's a square, it's a rectangle” and “if it's not a rectangle, it's not a square” say the same thing. The converse is not: not every rectangle is a square. Check both in the lab.

Laws to keep

Rewriting conditionals

Contrapositive lawP → Q ≡ ¬Q → ¬P

The basis of proof by contrapositive.

Conditional lawsP → Q ≡ ¬P ∨ Q
¬(P → Q) ≡ P ∧ ¬Q

The second tells you what a counterexample to an if–then looks like: P true, Q false.

BiconditionalP ↔ Q ≡ (P → Q) ∧ (Q → P)

“P if and only if Q”, often shortened to “P iff Q”. Prove it in two halves.
