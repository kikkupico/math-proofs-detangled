A ∪ B combines two sets. But what about ten sets, or infinitely many? A family is a set whose elements are sets, and its union and intersection are defined with quantifiers instead of connectives.

x ∈ ⋃ℱ  means  ∃A ∈ ℱ (x ∈ A)
in at least one set of the family
x ∈ ⋂ℱ  means  ∀A ∈ ℱ (x ∈ A)
in every set of the family

An indexed family

Often the sets in a family come with labels. Here Ai is the set of multiples of i between 1 and 36, and you choose the index set I.

⋃i∈I Ai collects numbers that are multiples of some chosen i. ⋂i∈I Ai keeps numbers that are multiples of all of them: the common multiples.

Infinite families

Every interval is nonempty. Their intersection is empty.

Let An = (0, 1/n) for each positive integer n. Is any real number in all of them?

For any x > 0, pick n with 1/n < x. Then x ∉ An. And numbers ≤ 0 are in none. So ⋂n An = ∅, even though every single An has infinitely many elements. Meanwhile ⋃n An = (0, 1).

Notice the proof. To show x ∉ ⋂, you negate a ∀, which gives an ∃: you find one set that misses x.

Two ways to write the same thing

A family ℱℱ = {{1, 2, 3}, {2, 3, 4}, {3, 4, 5}}

⋃ℱ = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5} and ⋂ℱ = {3}.

Indexed by IA1 = {1, 2, 3}, A2 = {2, 3, 4}, A3 = {3, 4, 5}

With I = {1, 2, 3}, ⋃i∈I Ai is the same union. Here x ∈ ⋃i∈I Ai means ∃i ∈ I (x ∈ Ai).
