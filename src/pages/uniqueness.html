“There is exactly one” makes two separate claims. Existence: at least one object has the property. Uniqueness: at most one does, because any two that have it must be equal. Prove both, in two clearly labelled parts.

Existence. Let x = [witness]. … check P(x) … Uniqueness. Suppose P(y) and P(z). … show y = z … ∎

∃!x P(x) unpacks on the Quantifier Equivalences page.

Example 1

There is exactly one positive integer n with n² = n

Existence: find a witness. n = 1 is positive and 1² = 1.

Uniqueness: take two arbitrary positive integers with the property, and show they're equal. Here it's easier to show each one must equal 1.

Rearrange: m² = m means m(m − 1) = 0, so m = 0 or m = 1. It's positive, so m = 1. Same for k.

Both parts done.

Existence. 1 is a positive integer and 1² = 1. Uniqueness. Suppose m and k are positive integers with m² = m and k² = k. From m² = m we get m(m − 1) = 0, so m = 0 or m = 1; since m > 0, m = 1. Likewise k = 1. So m = k. ∎
Example 2

Exactly one set A has A ∪ B = B for every set B

Existence is easy: ∅ ∪ B = B for every B.

Uniqueness has a lovely trick. Suppose A and A′ both have the property. Use each one's property with the other one plugged in as B. That's the ∀-given move: you may plug in anything you like.

A has the property for every B. Plug in B = A′.

A′ has the property for every B. Plug in B = A.

Union doesn't care about order, so the two left sides are the same set.

A ∪ A′ = A′
A′ ∪ A = A
A′ = A ∪ A′ = A′ ∪ A = A
Common slip

Proving uniqueness by showing your witness is the only one you could think of. Uniqueness needs an argument about arbitrary objects with the property: suppose y and z both work, then derive y = z.
