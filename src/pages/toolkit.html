When nothing fits

Two moves work whatever the statement looks like. Reach for them when the move for your form doesn't get you anywhere.

Proof by contradiction

Assume the goal is false.

Add its negation to what you know and follow the consequences until two of them can't both be true. This works especially well when the goal is negative, such as “not rational” or “infinitely many”.

Proof by cases

Split the possibilities.

Break the situation into cases that together cover everything, such as n even and n odd, and prove the goal in each one. Missing a case breaks the proof.

  • n is even

  • n is odd
I shall make no assertion before its time. The motto of Chapter 3 (p. 86). Assume whatever a move allows, but claim nothing you can't yet justify.

The routine

This is how the book works out every proof in Chapter 3. The order matters, so the steps are numbered.

1

Write it down

List what you know and what you're proving. At the start, that's the theorem's hypotheses and its conclusion.

2

Read the goal's shape

Apply the move for its outermost symbol. Repeat as the goal changes.

3

Unpack definitions

When the goal has no symbol left to work on, write out what words like “divides”, “⊆” or “onto” mean.

4

Use what you know

Check each given for a move: plug into ∀, name what ∃ promises, split ∧, and look for modus ponens.

5

Write the clean version

Turn it into ordinary sentences. Keep the justifications and drop the scratch work.

After the book

A selection from the book's own list of further reading (p. 375), grouped by where you might go next.

Problem solving

Pólya, How to Solve It

The classic on how to approach problems, which fits well with the scratch-work habits from Chapter 3.

Logic

Enderton · Mendelson · Hamilton

Mathematical logic proper. Also Barwise & Etchemendy, Language, Proof and Logic.

Sets & infinity

Halmos, Naive Set Theory

Then Enderton's Elements of Set Theory, which goes beyond Chapter 7.

Discrete mathematics

Epp · Rosen · Ross & Wright

Broader courses that use these proof skills on graphs, counting and algorithms.

Number theory

Burton · Stark

Elementary number theory, where the Introduction's questions about primes come from.
