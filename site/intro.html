Why prove anything?

Because patterns lie.

The book opens with prime numbers and a pattern that holds for every case you check, until it doesn't. Only a proof can tell you whether a pattern will keep going.

Key ideas

A conjecture

Up to 10, 2ⁿ−1 is prime exactly when n is.

A guess based on examples is called a conjecture. This one looks very convincing.

2n − 1
A counterexample

One case is enough to sink it.

11 is prime, but 2¹¹ − 1 = 2047 = 23 · 89. Half of the conjecture is false.

2047 = 23 · 89
The catch

No amount of examples proves a theorem.

The other half survives every check up to 30. That still doesn't show it's true, because the next case could be the first failure.

?
A proof

If n isn't prime, 2ⁿ−1 isn't either.

The proof gives a recipe that turns any factorization of n into a factorization of 2ⁿ−1.

n = ab ⇒ 2b−1 divides
Euclid, c. 350 B.C.

There are infinitely many primes.

Multiply any finite list of primes, add 1, and the result has a prime factor that isn't on the list.

Prime deserts

Gaps between primes can be as long as you like.

For any n, there are n consecutive numbers with no prime among them.

(n+1)! + 2, …
Try it

Conjecture, counterexample, theorem

The book states two conjectures. Conjecture 1: if n is prime, so is 2ⁿ−1. Conjecture 2: if n is not prime, neither is 2ⁿ−1.

Drag the slider to check more cases and watch each conjecture's status.

Mersenne checker

A proof is a recipe

Here is the idea behind Conjecture 2, which becomes a theorem. If n = ab, let x = 2b − 1 and y = 1 + 2b + 22b + ⋯ + 2(a−1)b. Multiplying them out, almost every term cancels, and xy = 2n − 1.

The proof doesn't check cases. It works for every composite n at once. Pick one and watch.

Factor machine

Euclid's machine

Suppose someone hands you a list of primes and says it's complete. Multiply them all together and add 1. Dividing that number by anything on the list leaves remainder 1. But it does have prime factors, so at least one prime is missing from the list.

The book's version is a proof by contradiction. Chapter 3 takes it apart step by step.

Pick a “complete” list

The new prime may be bigger or smaller than the ones on your list. Try 2, 5, 11: you get 111 = 3 · 37.

Deserts with no primes

Primes thin out as numbers grow. There are 25 below 100, but only six between 1,000,000 and 1,000,100. The book proves you can find a stretch of n consecutive non-primes for any n.

Start at (n+1)! + 2. Each of the next n numbers has an obvious factor: the first is divisible by 2, the next by 3, and so on.

Desert generator

Easy to ask. Still open

The Introduction ends with famous questions about primes. They're simple to state, and as far as the book knows, nobody has answered them yet.

Mersenne primes

Are there infinitely many primes of the form 2ⁿ−1?

They are named after Marin Mersenne (1588–1647). Many of the largest known primes are of this form. When the book was written in April 2005, the record was 225,964,951 − 1, a number with 7,816,230 digits.

Perfect numbers
6 = 1+2+3

A number is perfect if it equals the sum of its smaller divisors. 28 is the next one.

Euclid + Euler

Every Mersenne prime makes a perfect number.

If 2ⁿ−1 is prime, then 2ⁿ⁻¹(2ⁿ−1) is perfect. About 2000 years later Euler proved that every even perfect number arises this way. Nobody knows whether an odd perfect number exists.

Twin primes

5 and 7. 29 and 31. 7949 and 7951.

Pairs of primes that differ by 2 keep turning up. Are there infinitely many of them? It is not known.
