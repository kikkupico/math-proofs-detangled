Connectives

Not, and, or: the atoms of reasoning.

Complicated statements are built from simple ones, glued together with a handful of words: not, and, or. Giving each word a symbol and one exact meaning is the first step toward seeing the shape of any statement.

¬

not

∧

and

∨

or

Try it

Flip the switches

Let P stand for “it's raining” and Q for “it's cold”. Each is either true or false. Flip them and watch the compound statements respond.

¬P is true exactly when P is false. P ∧ Q needs both. P ∨ Q needs at least one, and is still true when both are. That last point trips people up: in mathematics, “or” is always inclusive.

Translating

English has many words for the same connective

Logic ignores tone and keeps only the truth conditions. “But” and “although” carry surprise in English; logically they're just “and”.

and

“P but Q”, “P although Q”, “P, yet Q”, “both P and Q”

P ∧ Q
neither … nor

“Neither P nor Q” means not P and not Q.

¬P ∧ ¬Q
not both

“Not both P and Q” allows one of them, just not the two together.

¬(P ∧ Q)
exclusive or

“P or Q but not both”. Mathematics spells this out when it means it.

(P ∨ Q) ∧ ¬(P ∧ Q)
Building a formula, piece by piece

Start with the sentence: “I'll have neither cake nor ice cream, but I'll have coffee.”

Name the simple statements. C: I'll have cake. I: I'll have ice cream. K: I'll have coffee.

“Neither C nor I” is ¬C ∧ ¬I.

“But” is just ∧. Put brackets round the first part so it's clear what's being joined.

neither cake nor ice cream, but coffee
¬C ∧ ¬I
(¬C ∧ ¬I) ∧ K
Brackets matter

Where the ¬ goes

¬P ∨ Q and ¬(P ∨ Q) are different statements. The first says “not P, or Q”. The second says “neither P nor Q”.

The convention: ¬ applies to the smallest thing right after it. To negate something bigger, put it in brackets. When in doubt, add brackets; they never hurt.
