Key ideas

The model

Givens and a goal.

At every moment, a proof-in-progress is a list of things you know and one thing you want. Each strategy changes the list.

Givens | Goal
The motto

“I shall make no assertion before its time.”

Never claim something until the givens fully justify it. Assuming something for the sake of argument is allowed, as long as you know it's an assumption.

⊢
If–then

To prove P → Q, assume P and prove Q.

Or prove the contrapositive: assume ¬Q, prove ¬P.

P → Q
For all

To prove ∀x P(x), let x be arbitrary.

Assume nothing about x except what the theorem gives you, then prove P(x).

∀x
There exists

To prove ∃x P(x), find one.

Name a specific value and check it works. Scratch work is where you hunt for it. The final proof just shows it.

∃x
Stuck?

Assume the opposite.

Proof by contradiction: suppose the goal is false and follow the consequences until two of them can't both be true.

⊥

Two kinds of writing

Scratch work is where you think. You list the givens and the goal, then apply strategies that change them. That's how Velleman works out every example in the chapter.

The final proof is what you hand in. It keeps only the steps that justify the conclusion, written as ordinary English. It explains why the theorem is true, not how you thought of the argument.

The book shows both for each example. Try the scratch work yourself before you read the final proof.

Example 3.1.2 · p. 880 < a < b ⇒ a² < b²

Givens (after)

  • a, b are real
  • 0 < a < b

Goal (after)

  • a² < b²

Final proof, in our words: Suppose 0 < a < b. Multiplying a < b by the positive number a gives a² < ab. Multiplying by b gives ab < b². So a² < ab < b². Therefore, if 0 < a < b then a² < b². ∎

I shall make no assertion before its time. The chapter's motto, p. 86. An assertion claims something is true and must be justified. An assumption only asks what would follow if it were true.

      Four moves you'll use everywhere

      They work on any goal. You'll meet them throughout the rest of the book.

      Contrapositive · §3.1

      Prove “if not Q, then not P”.

      P → Q and ¬Q → ¬P are equivalent, so you can prove whichever is easier. To show that if n² is even then n is even, suppose n is odd, say n = 2k+1. Then n² = 2(2k²+2k)+1 is odd.

      Contradiction · §3.2

      Assume the goal is false.

      This works best when the goal is a negative statement such as “not prime” or “infinitely many”, which means “not finitely many”. Euclid's proof that there are infinitely many primes is the classic example: assume a complete finite list, then build a number that breaks it.

      Cases · §3.5

      Split the world, cover every part.

      If you know P ∨ Q, prove the goal once assuming P and once assuming Q. For example, to show n² + n is even, take n even and n odd separately. The cases must be exhaustive: together they cover every possibility.

      Existence & uniqueness · §3.6

      Exactly one = at least one + at most one.

      To prove ∃!x P(x), show something works, then show that any two things that work are equal. Example: exactly one set A satisfies A ∪ B = B for every set B. Existence: ∅ works. Uniqueness: if A and A′ both work, then A′ = A ∪ A′ = A.

      §3.7 · p. 155

      The intro, decoded

      The three proofs that looked mysterious in the Introduction come back at the end of the chapter. Read with the strategies in mind, each one turns out to be a short sequence of standard moves.

      Theorem 3.7.1

      If n isn't prime, neither is 2n−1.

      Moves: “not prime” rewritten as a positive statement (there is a factorization). A ∃ given lets you name the factors a, b. A ∃ goal means you build the factors x = 2b−1 and y.

      Theorem 3.7.2

      There are infinitely many primes.

      Moves: the goal is negative (“not finitely many”), so suppose there is a complete finite list and aim for a contradiction. Then use cases: the number p₁⋯pₙ + 1 is either prime or a product of primes.

      Theorem 3.7.3

      Prime-free runs of any length.

      Moves: a ∀ goal, so let n be arbitrary. Then a ∃ goal, so build the run: start at (n+1)! + 2 and show that each of the next n numbers has a factor.
