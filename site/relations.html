Relations

Connected, precisely.

A relation is just a set of ordered pairs. From that one idea come orders, closures, and equivalence relations, which are how mathematicians say “the same, as far as we care”.

Key ideas

Ordered pairs

(a, b) ≠ (b, a).

Unlike the set {a, b}, a pair has a first and a second entry. (a, b) = (c, d) exactly when a = c and b = d.

( , )
Cartesian product

A × B is every pair you can make.

All (a, b) with a ∈ A and b ∈ B. If A has 3 elements and B has 2, A × B has 6.

Relations

A relation is any subset of A × B.

Write xRy for (x, y) ∈ R. “Less than”, “divides” and “is a parent of” are all relations.

Properties

Reflexive. Symmetric. Transitive.

Plus antisymmetric. Different combinations of these four give the important kinds of relation.

Partial orders

Like ⊆, where some pairs just aren't comparable.

Reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive. If every pair is comparable, it's a total order, like ≤.

⪯
Equivalence

Equivalence classes split a set into pieces.

Reflexive, symmetric and transitive relations sort a set into non-overlapping groups called a partition.

§4.1–4.2

Relations are sets, so you can operate on them

Because a relation is a set of pairs, everything from Chapters 1–2 applies to it. On top of that, there are three new operations.

Domain & range
Dom(R), Ran(R)

The first entries that actually appear in R, and the second entries.

Inverse
R⁻¹

Flip every pair: (b, a) ∈ R⁻¹ iff (a, b) ∈ R. The inverse of “parent of” is “child of”.

Composition
S ∘ R

(a, c) ∈ S ∘ R when some b has aRb and bSc. Go one step by R, then one by S.

Theorem 4.2.5

The algebra behaves.

Inverting twice gets you back to R. Composition is associative. And the inverse of a composition reverses the order: (S ∘ R)⁻¹ = R⁻¹ ∘ S⁻¹, just as you take off your shoes before your socks.

Verdict

Antisymmetric means: if xRy and yRx then x = y. That is not the same as “not symmetric”. The empty relation is both symmetric and antisymmetric.

§4.4

Orders, without the assumption that everything lines up

In a partial order, two elements can be incomparable. Under ⊆, neither {1} nor {2} is a subset of the other. That small gap means some familiar words need more careful definitions.

One result ties this chapter to Chapter 2. For a nonempty family F of subsets of A, ordered by ⊆, the least upper bound is ⋃F and the greatest lower bound is ⋂F (Theorem 4.4.11).

Smallest

Below every element. There's at most one.

Minimal

Nothing is strictly below it. There can be several.

Upper bound

An element above every member of a subset B. It doesn't have to be in B.

Least upper bound

The smallest of the upper bounds, if there is one.

§4.5

Closures: the smallest fix

If R isn't transitive, can you add pairs until it is? Yes, and there is a smallest way to do it. The book proves the reflexive, symmetric and transitive closures always exist.

Reflexive closure: R ∪ iA. Symmetric closure: R ∪ R⁻¹. The transitive closure is harder. Chapter 6 shows it equals R ∪ R² ∪ R³ ∪ ⋯, every pair connected by a path.

Example

R = {(1,2), (2,3), (3,4)}

transitive closure = R ∪ {(1,3), (2,4), (1,4)}

Load the “path” preset in the lab above and press “+ Transitive closure” to see the new pairs added.

§4.6

Same, for our purposes

An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. Its equivalence classes [x]R collect everything related to x. Together the classes form A/R.

Theorem 4.6.4: A/R is always a partition. Every element lands in exactly one class. Theorem 4.6.6 goes the other way: every partition comes from an equivalence relation.

The standard example is congruence mod m: x ≡ y (mod m) when m | (x − y).

ℤ / ≡ mod m
