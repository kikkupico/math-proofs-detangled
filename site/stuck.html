When You're Stuck

Small cases, working backwards, invariants.

The techniques tell you what to do once you can see the shape of a proof. Sometimes you can't. Then it helps to have a few strategies for exploring. They come from George Pólya's How to Solve It, still the classic on the subject.

1

Understand the problem

What's given? What's the goal? Try an example.

 2

Make a plan

Small cases, work backwards, look for an invariant.

 3

Carry it out

Write it as a proof, checking each step.

 4

Look back

Is every hypothesis used? Does it generalise?
Strategy · try small cases

Compute, guess, then prove

What is 1 + 3 + 5 + ⋯ + (2n − 1), the sum of the first n odd numbers? Don't think yet. Look at the table, then type a formula in n that you think fits.

A formula that matches every row is still only a guess: patterns lie. But now you know what to prove, and induction can prove it.

Strategy · work backwards

Start from the goal

Ask: what would I need to know to get the goal? Then what would I need to get that? Keep going until you reach something you have. Then write the proof forwards. The ε–δ example finds its δ exactly this way.

Goal

x + 1/x ≥ 2 for x > 0

⇐ need

x² + 1 ≥ 2x (multiply by x > 0)

⇐ need

(x − 1)² ≥ 0 (true!)

Now reverse it: “Since (x − 1)² ≥ 0, x² + 1 ≥ 2x. Dividing by x > 0 gives x + 1/x ≥ 2.” Each backwards step must be reversible, which is why the reasons matter. Compare mistake 1.

Strategy · find an invariant

Something that never changes

Remove two opposite corners from a chessboard. Can 31 dominoes, each covering two neighbouring squares, cover the remaining 62 squares?

Trying placements gets nowhere: there are far too many. Instead, notice what every domino does: it covers one dark square and one light square. So any tiling covers equally many of each. Count what's left.

Tap squares to remove or restore them, and watch the count.

More strategies

When those don't work

Specialise

Make it smaller

Prove it for n = 2, or for one particular set, or with an extra assumption. The special case often shows the general idea.

Generalise

Make it bigger

Sometimes a stronger statement is easier, especially for induction: a stronger hypothesis gives you more to use in the step.

Extremal

Take the smallest

Look at the smallest counterexample, the largest element, the shortest path. Its extreme-ness is extra information. See well-ordering.

Related problem

Have you seen it before?

A theorem with the same conclusion, or the same kind of unknown, may hand you the method.

Draw it

Make a picture

Venn diagrams for sets, a number line for inequalities, a graph for a function. Then turn what you see into words.

Look back

After it works

Was every hypothesis needed? Can the proof be shorter? What else does the method prove?
