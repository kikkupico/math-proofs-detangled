Strong Induction and Well-Ordering

Lean on every smaller case, not just the last one.

Ordinary induction lets you use P(n) to prove P(n + 1). Strong induction lets you use every earlier case: P(0), P(1), …, P(n − 1) are all available when you prove P(n). Use it when the case you need isn't the one just before.

Let n be arbitrary, and suppose P(k) holds for every k < n. (strong hypothesis) … show P(n), using any smaller case … By strong induction, ∀n P(n). ∎

No separate base case is written: for the smallest n there are no smaller cases, so the proof must handle it directly. Watch for that.

Why ordinary induction gets stuck

12 doesn't care about 11

Claim: every integer n ≥ 2 is a product of primes. To handle 12, knowing that 11 is a product of primes is no help at all. What helps is 12 = 2 · 6, and then 6 = 2 · 3.

The cases you need are smaller, but not one smaller. Strong induction gives you all of them at once.

The same idea, turned around

Well-ordering: take the smallest counterexample

Well-ordering principle: every nonempty set of natural numbers has a smallest element. It is equivalent to induction, and it suggests a style of proof by contradiction.

Suppose the claim fails. Then the set of counterexamples is nonempty, so it has a smallest member m. Everything below m works. Now show m works too, or find a smaller counterexample. Either way, contradiction.

The “lowest terms” step in the √2 proof is exactly this: pick the fraction for √2 with the smallest denominator.

Claim: every integer n ≥ 2 has a prime factor. Suppose not.

The counterexamples form a nonempty set. Take the smallest, m.

m isn't prime (a prime is its own prime factor), so m = ab with 2 ≤ a < m.

a is smaller than m, so it's not a counterexample: it has a prime factor p. Then p | a | m. So m has one after all.

Suppose some integer ≥ 2 has no prime factor. By well-ordering, let m be the smallest such integer. m is not prime, so m = ab with 2 ≤ a < m. By minimality, a has a prime factor p, and p | m. Contradiction. ∎
