Chapter 6 · pp. 260–305

Mathematical Induction

Infinitely many cases. Two steps.

Prove it for 0. Prove that each case passes to the next. Then every natural number falls, like a row of dominoes.

Knock them overMeet strong induction
Base case
P(0)
Induction step
∀n ∈ ℕ (P(n) → P(n+1))

Key ideas

The principle

Base case plus induction step proves ∀n ∈ ℕ P(n).

In this book ℕ starts at 0. The step is a proof of an if–then statement, so Chapter 3's strategies apply: let n be arbitrary and assume P(n).

0 → 1 → 2 → ⋯
First example

2⁰ + 2¹ + ⋯ + 2ⁿ = 2ⁿ⁺¹ − 1.

Adding 2ⁿ⁺¹ to both sides of the formula for n gives the formula for n + 1.

1+2+4+8 = 15
Flexible start

The base case doesn't have to be 0.

2ⁿ > n² is false for n = 2, 3 and 4. Starting at n = 5 proves it for every n ≥ 5.

2n > n²
Recursion

Recursion defines. Induction proves.

0! = 1 and (n+1)! = (n+1)·n! defines n! for every n. Proofs about n! follow the same pattern.

n!
Strong induction

Assume every smaller case, not just the previous one.

That's what you need when n splits into pieces much smaller than n − 1, such as its factors.

∀k < n P(k)
Well-ordering

Every nonempty set of naturals has a smallest element.

It's equivalent to induction, and it gives a short proof that √2 is irrational.

√2 ∉ ℚ
Try it · §6.1

Why you need both steps

Domino n falling means P(n) is proved. Push the first one. Then remove the base case, or break the induction step at one link, and see how far the proof gets.

Example 6.1.1

Watch the step happen

Base case: 2⁰ = 1 = 2¹ − 1.

Induction step: suppose 2⁰ + ⋯ + 2n = 2n+1 − 1. Add 2n+1 to both sides. The right side becomes 2 · 2n+1 − 1 = 2n+2 − 1, which is the formula for n + 1.

The picture shows why: each new block is as big as all the earlier ones together, plus one.

2⁰ + 2¹ + ⋯ + 2ⁿ

The thin bar below is 2ⁿ⁺¹ long. The sum falls short of it by exactly one unit, the empty slot.

§6.2

Induction in disguise

Many goals don't look like ∀n ∈ ℕ P(n). The trick is to find a number hidden in the problem and induct on it.

The book's examples: every finite nonempty set in a partial order has a minimal element (induct on its size), and every partial order on a finite set extends to a total order. For a polygon with corners on a circle, the angles add to (n − 2) · 180°.

§6.4

Strong induction: lean on every smaller case

To show every integer above 1 is prime or a product of primes (Theorem 6.4.2), take n. If it's prime, you're done. If not, n = ab with a and b smaller than n. They aren't necessarily n − 1, so ordinary induction doesn't help. Strong induction lets you assume every smaller number already factors.

This is the fact the Introduction borrowed for Euclid's proof. Here it finally gets a proof.

§6.3–6.4

Recursion, and a formula that shouldn't work

The Fibonacci numbers are defined recursively: F0 = 0, F1 = 1, and each later term is the sum of the two before it. Since each term depends on two earlier ones, proofs about them need strong induction.

Theorem 6.4.3 gives a closed formula full of √5 that somehow always produces a whole number.

Fₙ = ((1+√5)/2)ⁿ − ((1−√5)/2)ⁿ, divided by √5

More tools in this chapter

Theorem 6.4.1 · Division algorithm

Divide, get a remainder.

For natural numbers n and m with m > 0, there are natural numbers q and r with n = mq + r and r < m. The proof uses strong induction on n. (Uniqueness of q and r is left to the exercises.)

Theorem 6.4.4 · Well-ordering

There's always a smallest one.

Every nonempty set of natural numbers has a smallest element. A common way to use it: suppose there's a counterexample, take the smallest one, and show an even smaller one exists.

Theorem 6.4.5

√2 is irrational.

If √2 = p/q, choose the smallest such q. The algebra then produces a fraction equal to √2 with a smaller denominator, which contradicts the choice of q.

§6.5 · Closures again

Loose ends from earlier chapters.
