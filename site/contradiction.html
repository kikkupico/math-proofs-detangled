Proof by Contradiction

Assume the opposite and watch it break.

Some goals are negative: “x ≠ 3”, “there is no largest integer”, “√2 is not rational”. They give you nothing to aim at. Proof by contradiction flips them: assume the goal is false, and follow the consequences until two of them clash.

Suppose, for contradiction, ¬Q. … use it as a new given … … reach some R … … and also ¬R. Contradiction. So Q. ∎

It works because a true assumption can never lead to a contradiction. So the assumption ¬Q was false.

Example 1

There is no largest integer

The goal is a negative statement: ¬∃n ∈ ℤ ∀m ∈ ℤ (m ≤ n). Assume the opposite: some integer N is the largest. Then N + 1 is an integer bigger than N. Contradiction.

Try to be the person who claims a largest integer exists. Every candidate gets beaten the same way, and that “same way” is the proof.

The outer shape is P → Q, so first assume the hypothesis. That leaves the negative goal x ≠ 3.

Assuming x = 3 gives you something concrete to calculate with. Notice the goal changes too: it's no longer “x ≠ 3” but “reach any contradiction”.

When to use it

Good signs

≠ ∉ ¬

A negative goal

“Not equal”, “not in”, “not rational”, “no largest”. Assuming the positive version gives you something to work with.

∞

“Infinitely many”

Means “not finitely many”, a negative. Assume a finite list and break it. That's Euclid's proof.

√2

Irrationality

“Irrational” means “not a fraction”. Assume it is one and find a contradiction. See √2 is irrational.

Common slip

Reaching a contradiction by a mistake in algebra. If your “contradiction” came easily, check every step. Also: if you assumed ¬Q and then proved Q directly without using the assumption, you didn't need contradiction at all; rewrite it as a direct proof.
